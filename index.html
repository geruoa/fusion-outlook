Seeing California's water and crop risks weeks to seasons ahead

We combine long observational records with AI weather models to answer three practical questions for California's water and agriculture sectors: what has already changed, what can be predicted 2 to 6 weeks out, and how those outlooks reach the people who make decisions.

How the work fits together

One line of work, from diagnosis to decision

Each study answers the question the previous one raises. The first shows that California's precipitation regime has shifted. The second asks how far ahead the main source of 2–6 week predictability can be forecast. The third shows what variability means for orchard crops and tests an in-season forecast. The fourth asks what makes growers and advisors use tools like these.

Summary

For water and agriculture agencies

What the results mean for planning now

  • Baselines

    Use a recent ~20-year climatology

    Mean precipitation and its variability have both declined since the late 1990s. A recent 20-year baseline describes today's hydroclimate better than a fixed 30-year normal, which can move drought triggers, rule curves and allocations. Compare baselines

  • Storms

    Plan for fewer large storms

    Extreme-wet months now supply less of the annual total. That raises the value of carryover storage and readiness to capture recharge when big storms do arrive.

  • Climate modes

    Use ENSO, PDO and similar indices for direction, not for wet-year size

    Climate modes and their interactions reproduce where drying occurs but explain only about 25% of year-to-year swings in wet months.

  • Weeks 3–6

    Use MJO outlooks when the signal is strong

    AI-based MJO forecasts stay skillful through 42 days when they predict a strong MJO, against about 24 days when they predict a weak one. Check a forecast

  • Orchards

    Decide on dormancy treatments with an end-of-January chill check

    Observed chill through January plus a 1-month ECMWF forecast for February lands within 10% of the season total in 41–42 of 43 years. See the risk

For funders and program officers

Why this is worth investing in

  • New benchmark

    State-of-the-art MJO skill from an AI foundation model

    A lightweight correction model extends skillful MJO prediction from 37 to 40 days, compared with about 30 days for operational systems such as ECMWF. AI models also run orders of magnitude faster than physics models once trained.

  • Better methods

    Clear guidance on where gains come from

    Longer MJO history does not add skill, and much of the improvement credited to bias correction in earlier work reflects dataset mismatch. Effort is better spent on the initial atmospheric state and new training objectives.

  • Near-term risk

    Ag impacts are arriving earlier than projections say

    Rising variability already produces chill shortfalls that CMIP6 models, with about half the observed variability, mostly miss. Mid-century timelines understate the urgency for breeding and orchard planning.

  • Adoption

    Tools are adopted for usefulness, not belief

    Belief in climate change does not predict tool use. Tools that fit existing decisions and reach growers through advisors can serve users across political views.

  • Next steps

    Ready to extend

    Open questions the team is positioned to pursue: how often MJO forecasts produce false alarms for California precipitation, how to separate natural and human drivers of the drying, and how to extend skillful chill outlooks beyond one month.

Team and funding

Team effort

The project "A fusion outlook product for predicting climate water variation toward efficient decision making" is led by Isabella Velicogna and Geruo A at UC Irvine, with research partners at UC Merced and UC Davis, end-user partners at the California Department of Water Resources and Salt River Project, and collaborators in agriculture, industry and Earth system modeling.
